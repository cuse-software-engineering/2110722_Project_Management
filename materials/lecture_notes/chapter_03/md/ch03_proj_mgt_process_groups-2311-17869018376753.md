## Slide 3: Project Management Process Groups (1 of 2)

- A process is a series of actions directed toward a particular result
    - Project management can be viewed as a number of related processes
- Project management process groups
    - Initiating processes
    - Planning processes
    - Executing processes
    - Monitoring and controlling processes
    - Closing processes

## Slide 4: Project Management Process Groups (1 of 4)

- Initiating processes
    - Defining and authorizing a project or project phase
    - Identifying stakeholders
    - E.g. In finishing phase, ensuring that the team completes all work, someone documents lessons learned, relevant stakeholders are prepared
- Planning processes
    - Devising and maintaining a workable scheme, e.g. scope/schedule/cost/procurement management plans
    - Revising plans during each phase to account for changing conditions
- Executing processes
    - Coordinating people and other resources to carry out plans
    - Creating products, services, or results of the project or phase
    - E.g. managing project work, managing project knowledge, acquiring resources, conducting procurements

## Slide 5: Project Management Process Groups (2 of 4)

- Monitoring and controlling processes
    - Monitoring and measuring progress against plans
    - Taking corrective actions when necessary
    - E.g. reporting performance, identifying necessary changes
- Closing processes
    - Formalizing acceptance of the project or project phase and ending it
    - E.g. archiving project files, documenting lessons learned, receiving formal acceptance of the delivered work

## Slide 6: Project Management Process Groups (3 of 4)

- Process groups are not mutually exclusive
    - E.g. monitoring and controlling occurs concurrently with other process groups throughout the project
- Level of activity and length of each process group varies for every project
    - Executing normally requires the most resources and time, followed by planning
- Process groups can be applied to an entire project (i.e. waterfall) or to each phase or iteration of a project (i.e. agile)

- Use agile when projects have
    - Less rigid constraints

## Slide 35: Scrum Roles, Artifacts, and Ceremonies (1 of 4)

- Scrum roles
    - Product owner
        - person responsible for the business value of the project and for deciding what work to do and in what order, as documented in the product backlog
    - ScrumMaster
        - person who ensures that the team is productive, facilitates the daily Scrum, enables close cooperation across all roles and functions, and removes barriers that prevent the team from being effective
        - authority over process but not the people on the team
    - Developer
        - cross-functional team who organize themselves and the work to produce the desired results for each sprint, which normally lasts 2-4 weeks
    - All roles form a Scrum team of 10 or fewer people

## Slide 36: Scrum Roles, Artifacts, and Ceremonies (2 of 4)

- An artifact is a work product of the process
- Scrum artifacts
    - Product backlog
        - list of features prioritized by business value
    - Sprint backlog
        - highest-priority items from the product backlog to be completed within a sprint
    - Burndown/Burnup chart
        - shows the cumulative work remaining/done in a sprint on a day-by-day basis

## Slide 37: Scrum Roles, Artifacts, and Ceremonies (3 of 4)

- Scrum ceremonies
    - Sprint planning session
        - meeting with the team to select a set of work from the product backlog to deliver during a sprint
        - Maximum eight hours; two hours for each week of sprint length
    - Daily Scrum
        - short meeting for the development team to share progress and challenges and plan work for the day
        - 15 minutes or less
    - Sprint reviews
        - meeting in which the team demonstrates to the product owner what it has completed during the sprint
        - Maximum four hours; one hour for each week of sprint length
    - Sprint retrospectives
        - meeting in which the team looks for ways to improve the product and the process based on a review of the actual performance of the development team
        - Maximum three hours; 45 minutes for each week of sprint length

    - Daily Scrum: held each morning to plan and communicate work for the day and discuss any risks, issues, or blockers which the ScrumMaster has to handle
    - Sprint review: work progress within a sprint can be represented on a sprint board maintained by the ScrumMaster
        - Sprint review demonstration to the product owner to show what has been completed during the sprint
        - Burndown chart: an important artifact used to graphically display progress on each sprint, plotting each day's sum of hours or story points remaining

## Slide 51: Project Closing

- After the sprint review, the ScrumMaster leads a sprint retrospective
    - Team reflects on what happened during the sprint, similar to a lessons-learned report but on a shorter period of time
- Sprint retrospective is intended to answer two fundamental questions
    - What went well during the last sprint that we should continue doing?
    - What could we do differently to improve the product or process?
- Suggested action items for implementation could be added to the product backlog if agreed by the product owner
