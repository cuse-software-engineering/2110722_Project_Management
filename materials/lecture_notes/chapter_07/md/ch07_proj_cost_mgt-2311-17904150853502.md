## Slide 4: The Importance of Project Cost Management

- IT projects have a poor track record for meeting budget goals
- Cost is a resource sacrificed to achieve a specific objective, or something given up in exchange
  - Usually measured in monetary units that must be paid to acquire goods and services
- Cost overrun is the additional percentage or monetary amount by which actual costs exceed estimates
  - May be caused by unclear project requirements, involving new technology or business processes
- Benefit is the value received from something, e.g. revenue from sales, ability to do something you could not perform before, removing defects
  - These may be converted into a currency value and compared to the cost

## Slide 5: Basic Principles of Cost Management (1 of 2)

- Types of costs and benefits
  - Tangible costs or benefits are those costs or benefits that an organization can easily measure in monetary terms
  - Intangible costs or benefits are costs or benefits that are difficult to measure in monetary terms
    - E.g. Costs not billed to the project
    - E.g. Goodwill, prestige
  - Direct costs are costs that can be directly related to producing the products and services of the project
    - E.g. Salaries, hardware/software cost
    - Project managers should focus on direct costs because they can be controlled
  - Indirect costs are costs that are not directly related to the products or services of the project, but are indirectly related to performing work on the project
    - E.g. electricity, water supply, paper towels
  - Sunk cost is money that has been spent in the past; consider it gone like a sunken ship that can never be raised
    - When deciding what projects to invest in or continue, you should not include sunk costs

## Slide 6: Basic Principles of Cost Management (2 of 2)

- Learning curve theory states that when many items are produced repetitively, the unit cost of those items decreases in a regular pattern as more units are produced
- Reserves are monetary amount included in a cost estimate to mitigate cost risk by allowing for future situations that are difficult to predict
  - Contingency reserves allow for future situations or identified risks that may be partially planned for (sometimes called known unknowns) and are included in the project cost baseline
    - E.g. If an organization knows it has 20% rate of turnover for IT personnel, it should include contingency reserves for recruiting and training costs of IT personnel
  - Management reserves allow for future situations or unidentified risks that are unpredictable (sometimes called unknown unknowns)
    - E.g. If a project manager gets sick or a supplier goes out of business, management reserves could be set aside for the resulting costs
    - Linked to an organization’s policy and a project’s level of uncertainty, available with higher management approval, and not included in the cost baseline

## Slide 7: Project Cost Management Processes

- Required to ensure that the project is completed within an approved budget

1. Planning cost management
   - Determining the policies, procedures, and documentation that will be used for planning, executing, and controlling project cost
2. Estimating costs
   - Developing an estimate of the costs of the resources needed to complete a project

   - Allocating the overall cost estimate to individual work items to establish a baseline for measuring performance

   - Controlling changes to the project budget

## Slide 9: 1. Planning Cost Management

- The first step in project cost management is planning how the costs will be managed throughout the life of the project
  - The project team uses expert judgment, analytical techniques, and meetings to develop the cost management plan
- Cost management plan includes:
  - Level of accuracy
    - E.g. rounding guideline (e.g. to the nearest $100), contingency funds (e.g. 10-20%)
  - Units of measure
    - E.g. Labor hours or days
  - Organizational procedure links
    - A selected WBS component becomes a point at which the organization tracks and controls project costs in its accounting system; this point is called a control account
    - It allows management to ask: For this portion of the WBS, how much did we plan, how much work have we accomplished, and how much have we actually spent?
  - Control thresholds
    - Amount of variation allowed before actions need to be taken, e.g. 10% of baseline cost
  - Rules of performance measurement
    - How often actual costs will be tracked and to what level of detail
  - Reporting formats
    - Format and frequency of cost reports
  - Process descriptions
    - How to perform all of the cost management processes

## Slide 12: Types of Cost Estimates (2 of 2)

- Estimates are usually done at various stages of a project
  - Should become more accurate as time progresses
- It is important to provide basis for the estimate, i.e. supporting details for estimates
  - Assumptions used in creating the estimate, e.g. labor rate, estimated effort
  - Cost estimation tools and techniques used, e.g. analogous method
- A large percentage of total project costs are often labor costs
  - FTE or full-time equivalent staff is a standard way to refer to the total staffing required regardless of whether people work full time or part time
    - E.g. when an employer has a 40-hour workweek, employees who are scheduled to work 40 hours per week are 1.0 FTEs. Employees scheduled to work 20 hours per week are 0.5 FTEs
  - Labor costs are often higher for contractors, so it is important to distinguish between internal and external resources

## Slide 13: Cost Estimation Tools and Techniques

- Expert judgment
- Analogous
  - Use the actual cost of a previous, similar project as the basis for estimating the cost of the current project
- Bottom-up estimates
  - Involve estimating individual work items or activities and summing them to get a project total (see later)
- Three-point estimates
  - Involve estimating the most likely, optimistic, and pessimistic costs for items
- Parametric estimating
  - Uses project characteristics (parameters) in a mathematical model to estimate project costs (see later)

## Slide 14: Typical Problems with IT Cost Estimates Inaccuracy

- Estimates are done too quickly
  - Many estimates must be done quickly before clear system requirements have been produced
  - Estimates are done at various stages, and project managers need to explain the rationale for each estimate
- People lack estimating experience
  - Not enough accurate reliable project data on which to base estimates are available
  - Organizations should keep reliable project information including estimates
- Human beings are biased toward underestimation
  - Senior project managers might make estimates based on their own abilities and forget that younger people will be working on the project
- Management desires accuracy
  - Management might ask for an estimate but really want a more accurate, and lower, number
  - Project managers must use leadership and negotiation skills to stand by the estimates

## Slide 24: Function Point Analysis (FPA)

*Parametric estimating*

- Purpose is to measure and provide functional size of software to clients and stakeholders
  - Logical size, as opposed to physical size like line of code or objects
- Function points (FP) are a unit measure for software
  - To compare program requirements, design, or final program size independent of chosen design and implementation details
  - To estimate the size of the final program by using a specific conversion table between Function Points and program code size (LOC per FP)
  - To estimate project effort (FP per person-month)
- First proposed in 1979 by Allan Albrecht and has become a standard for measuring software size (ISO 20926:2009)
- Currently maintained by IFPUG (International Function Point User Group https://ifpug.org/), *(IFPUG logo shown)*

## Slide 44: Calculating Adjusted Function Points

- Value Adjustment Factor (VAF) is based on 14 general system characteristics (GSCs) that rate the general functionality of the software
  - In the ISO standard for the IFPUG technique (ISO/IEC 20926), this step is optional
- Each characteristic has associated descriptions that help determine its degree of influence
  - 0: No influence
  - 1: Incidental
  - 2: Moderate
  - 3: Average
  - 4: Significant
  - 5: Essential

VAF = 0.65 + 0.01 * Σ(i=1..14) F_i ; VAF is in [0.65, 1.35]

Adjusted FP = Unadjusted FP * VAF

## Slide 53: 5 Scale Factors (1 of 3)

- Precedentedness (PREC)
  - If a product is similar to several previously developed projects, then the precedentedness is high.

- Team Cohesion (TEAM)

## Slide 66: Calendar Time to Develop

- TDEV is the amount of calendar time, in months, to develop the product
  - From determination of a product’s requirements baseline to completion of an acceptance activity
  - PM_NS = PM with nominal schedule, i.e. excluding SCED effort multiplier
  - C = TDEV coefficient, D = TDEV scaling base-exponent
  - SCED% = compression/expansion percentage in the SCED effort multiplier rating
  - A (in PM_NS), B, C, and D can be calibrated to organization's own database of projects. (It is recommended that at least A and C is calibrated to the local development environment.)

TDEV = [ C * (PM_NS)^(D + 0.2*(E − B)) ] * SCED% / 100
  where C = 3.67, D = 0.28, B = 0.91

E = B + 0.01 * Σ(j=1..5) SF_j   (same as PM calculation)

## Slide 73: Earned Value Management (EVM) (1 of 8)

- Project performance measurement technique that integrates scope, time, and cost data
  - Given a baseline (original plan plus approved changes), you can determine how well the project is meeting scope, time, and cost goals
- Earned value management involves calculating three values for each activity or summary activity from a project’s WBS at time t (monitoring time)
  - Planned value (PV)
  - Actual cost (AC)
  - Earned value (EV)
- Example: a project to purchase and install a new web server
  - Planned project duration = 2 weeks
  - Planned cost = $10,000 for labor hours, hardware, and software
  - Suppose time t = end of week1,
    - Completed work = 40%
    - Actual cost = $6,000

## Slide 74: Earned Value Management (EVM) (2 of 8)

- Budget at completion (BAC)
  - Original total budget for the project
  - BAC = $10,000
- Planned value (PV)
  - The authorized budget assigned to scheduled work at time t
  - Expected % of work completed at time t * BAC
  - PV = $5,000 (50% * 10,000)

  - Actual % of work completed at time t * BAC

## Slide 75: Earned Value Management (EVM) (3 of 8)

- Cost variance (CV)
  - Difference between the cost actually incurred for the work and the cost that should have incurred at time t
  - CV = EV-AC
  - If CV is negative, performing the work cost more than it should be
  - If CV is positive, performing the work cost less than it should be
  - CV = $-2,000
- Schedule variance (SV)
  - Difference between the monetary value of work completed and the planned value at time t
  - SV = EV-PV
  - If SV is negative, performing the work took longer than planned (behind schedule)
  - If SV is positive, performing the work took less time than planned (ahead of schedule)
  - SV = $-1,000

## Slide 76: Earned Value Management (EVM) (4 of 8)

- Cost performance index (CPI)
  - How efficient the actual cost spent is, compared to the monetary value of the completed work, determined at time t
  - CPI = EV/AC
  - If CPI is 1 or 100%, the actual cost is exactly as budgeted
  - If CPI is < 1 or 100%, the actual cost is over budget
  - If CPI is > 1 or 100% the actual cost is under budget
  - CPI = $4,000/6,000 = 0.67 (66.67%) = 33.33% over budget
    - The project team would not be able to complete the project within the original budget (BAC), if it continues to perform at the current cost efficiency (CPI)
- Schedule performance index (SPI)
  - How close the work is to being completed, compared to the amount of work that should be finished
  - SPI = EV/PV
  - If SPI is 1 or 100%, the work is on schedule
  - If SPI is < 1 or 100%, the work is behind schedule
  - If SPI is > 1 or 100%, the work is ahead of schedule
  - SPI = $4,000/5,000 = 0.8 (80%) = 20% behind schedule
    - The project team would not be able to complete the project within the original planned duration, if it continues to perform at the current schedule efficiency (SPI)

## Slide 77: Earned Value Management (EVM) (5 of 8)

- Estimate at completion (EAC)
  - An estimated cost of completing a project based on the performance to date, determined at time t
  - EAC = BAC/CPI
  - EAC = $10,000/0.67 = $14,925
- Estimated time to complete
  - An estimated time of completing a project based on the performance to date, determined at time t
  - Estimated time to complete = Original time estimate/SPI
  - Estimated time to complete = 2 weeks/0.8 = 2.5 weeks
- Estimated to complete (ETC)
  - The amount of money to spend more to complete the project based on the performance to date, determined at time t
  - ETC = EAC-AC
  - ETC = $14,925-6,000 = $8,925

## Slide 78: Earned Value Management (EVM) (6 of 8)

- To-complete performance index (TCPI)
  - The future cost performance index (future CPI) that you must follow for the remaining work if you want to complete the work within the given budget, i.e. BAC or EAC
  - TCPI = monetary value of remaining work/remaining funds
    - TCPI < 1 : project has more funds and less work, and it is easier to complete the project
    - TCPI = 1 : project has just enough funds to complete the work
    - TCPI > 1 : project has less funds and more work, and it is difficult to complete the project
  - To meet the BAC (i.e. to complete on original budget)
    - TCPI = (BAC-EV)/(BAC-AC)
    - TCPI = ($10,000-4,000)/($10,000-6,000) = $6,000/4,000 = 1.5
    - If the project team wants to complete the project within original budget (BAC), the future cost efficiency should be 1.5 (123.88% improvement from current CPI)
  - If it is clear that the BAC is not obtainable, project manager/team should develop a new budget forecast (EAC), with sponsor approval, by re-estimating the cost of the remaining work (ETC)

## Slide 79: Earned Value Management (EVM) (7 of 8)

- To-complete performance index (TCPI) (cont.)
  - If the sponsor is happy with the current cost performance (CPI), to meet the new budget EAC (slide 76)
    - TCPI = (BAC-EV)/(EAC-AC)
    - TCPI = ($10,000-4,000)/($14,925-6,000) = $6,000/8,925 = 0.67
  - If the sponsor is not happy with the current cost performance (CPI) and has imposed a new budget, to meet the new budget EAC
    - EAC = $12,000
    - ETC = EAC-AC = $12,000-6,000 = $6,000
    - TCPI = (BAC-EV)/(EAC-AC)
    - TCPI = ($10,000-4,000)/($12,000-6,000) = $6,000/6,000 = 1
    - If the project team wants to complete the project within the revised budget (EAC), the future cost efficiency should be 1 (49.25% improvement from current CPI)
  - TCPI is compared with CPI to determine if a target EAC is reasonable, e.g.
    - Reasonable: if TCPI differs from CPI within 0.5 of CPI
    - Overly Optimistic: if TCPI differs from CPI more than 0.5 of CPI
    - Overly Conservative: if CPI differs from TCPI more than 0.5 of TCPI
    - Anyway, it is still a judgment call
