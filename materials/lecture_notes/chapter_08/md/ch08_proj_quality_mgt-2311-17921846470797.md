## Slide 4: What Is Quality?

- International Organization for Standardization (ISO) definition of quality
  - “Totality of characteristics of an entity that bear on its ability to satisfy stated or implied needs” (ISO8402:1994)
  - “The degree to which a set of inherent characteristics fulfils requirements” (ISO9000:2000)
- Other definitions of quality
  - **Conformance to requirements**
    - Project’s processes and products meet written specifications
  - **Fitness for use**
    - Product can be used as it was intended

## Slide 5: Project Quality Management

- Project quality management ensures the project will satisfy the needs for which it was undertaken
- Project quality management processes

1. Planning quality management
   - **Identifying which quality standards** are relevant to the project, and how to satisfy them, e.g.
     - Allowing system growth,
     - Reasonable response time
     - Consistent and accurate information
     - Guaranteed service time
   - **Identifying metrics** as the standards of measurement, e.g.
     - Failure rate
     - Availability
     - Customer rating
2. Managing quality
   - Translating the quality management plan into **executable quality activities**
3. Controlling quality
   - Monitoring specific **project results to ensure they comply** with the relevant quality standards

## Slide 7: 1. Planning Quality Management (1 of 3)

  - Training and indoctrinating people in quality
  - Planning a process that ensures the appropriate outcome
- Project quality management planning

  - **Designs quality into the products** of the project

## Slide 11: Managing Quality (2 of 7)

- **Lean**
  - **Maximize customer value** while **minimizing waste**
  - Created at Toyota but owing its origin to Ford Company
  - Identify wasteful activities and the ways these wastes are killing business efficiency, and eliminate or reduce these wasteful activities

*(Illustrated pie-wheel of the 7 wastes, with a small green "VALUE ADDED" slice and a waste bin beside it)*

- VALUE ADDED
- OVERPRODUCTION — Creating too much material or information
- INVENTORY — Having more material or information than you need
- TRANSPORTATION — Moving material or information
- MOTION — Moving people to access or process material or information
- WAITING — Waiting for material or information, or material or information waiting to be processed
- DEFECTS — Errors or mistakes causing the effort to be redone to correct the problem
- OVERPROCESSING — Processing more than necessary to produce the desired output

## Slide 14: Managing Quality (5 of 7)

- **Kaizen**
  - Japanese word for **improvement** or **change for the better** *(kanji shown: 改善)*
  - First practiced in Japanese businesses, spearheaded by Toyota, after World War II
  - Influenced in part by American business and quality management experts
  - A mindset or philosophy, rather than a tool to use
    - Improving the business as a whole by creating a standard way of working, **increasing efficiency, and eliminating business waste**
    - **Collecting knowledge from all employees** within an organization to accomplish **incremental improvements on a regular basis**; achievement is not on any individual but collated as a whole

## Slide 15: Managing Quality (6 of 7)

- **Kanban**
  - Japanese term for signboard or billboard *(kanji shown: 看板)*
  - Developed in Japan by Toyota as a scheduling system for lean manufacturing and just-in-time manufacturing (**visual workflow**)
  - Uses visual cues for managing work as it moves through a process, **emphasizing continuous flow of work**
  - In agile software development, team can
    - Place **Kanban cards on the board to show status of work** in backlog such as to-do, in progress, validate, and complete
    - Impose **work-in-progress (WIP) limit** in any one workflow step at a time, e.g. development WIP limit = 3, testing WIP limit = 2
    - If development already contains 3 items, developers should not pull another item into development. Instead, they should help move existing work forward. This **prevents too much unfinished work from accumulating**.
    - **Continuous process** as there are no sprints
- Kanban board can be used with Scrum

## Slide 17: 3. Controlling Quality

- Main outputs of **quality control**
  - **Acceptance decisions**
    - Determine if the products or services produced as part of the project will be accepted or rejected
    - Accepted: validated deliverables
    - Rejected: rework
  - **Rework**
    - Action taken to bring rejected items to compliance with product requirements, specifications, and stakeholder expectations
    - Can be expensive, hence avoid this need by good quality planning and quality assurance
  - **Process adjustments**
    - Correct or prevent further quality problems
    - Often results in updates to organization process assets and project management plan

## Slide 19: Seven Basic Tools of Quality (1 of 22)

- **Cause and effect diagram (Fishbone or Ishikawa diagram)**
  - Created by Kaoru Ishikawa as a cause exploration tool
  - Can use **5 whys technique** to **peel away the symptoms and lead to the root cause** (other number can be used though)
    - The head of the fish → the **problem (effect)** you want to explain.
    - The bones → **main cause categories** (e.g. People-Methods-Machines-Materials-Environment-Measurement,  People-Software-Infrastructure-Process).
    - The smaller bones branching out → **underlying causes** (intermediate and root causes).
    - Root cause → The **deepest, most actionable cause** (you cannot further ask “why?”)
  - **For readability, use a separate table for 5 whys** analysis to trace selected causes that require deeper investigation to their root causes.

## Slide 22: Seven Basic Tools of Quality (4 of 22)

- **Control chart**
  - Illustrates the results of the process over time, i.e. **how stable the process is** and **whether the process creates defective products,** requiring corrective action
  - **Control limits** (Lower Control Limit (**LCL**) and Upper Control Limit (**UCL**)) are **calculated from process performance**, and are used to assess whether the process is stable and operating predictably.
    - When **process is in control**, variations in the results are created by random events, and no need to adjust the process.
      - Data points are within LCL and UCL.
    - When **process is out of control**, variations in the results are created by nonrandom events, and cause of the events are to be identified and the process adjusted.
      - Data points are outside LCL or UCL.
      - Seven Run occurs.
        - Seven consecutive data points are all below or all above the mean.
        - Seven consecutive data points are all increasing or decreasing.
  - **Specification limits** (Lower Specification Limit (**LSL**) and Upper Specification Limit (**USL**)) are **defined by customers** and are useful to see how capable the process is to meet customer requirements.
    - When **process is in specification**, the results meet customer requirements.
      - Data points are within LSL and USL.
    - When **process is out of specification**, the results are defective.
      - Data points are outside LSL or USL.

## Slide 23: Seven Basic Tools of Quality (5 of 22)

- **Control chart (cont.)**

*(Screenshot of a worked example; key phrases are underlined in green, shown here in bold)*

**Example: Applying Control Charts to Software Product Quality Control**

Consider a software development project building an **online hotel reservation system**. The project team uses a control chart to monitor the **response time of the hotel's room-search API**.

The objective is to determine **whether the software consistently meets its performance requirements** and **whether changes in performance indicate problems that require corrective action**.

**1. Define specification limits**

Suppose the performance requirement is:

> The room-search API should respond in 500 milliseconds, with an acceptable range of 400–600 milliseconds under a standardized workload.

| Parameter | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Target | 500 ms | Desired response time |
| Lower Specification Limit (LSL) | 400 ms | Minimum acceptable value for this exercise |
| Upper Specification Limit (USL) | 600 ms | Maximum acceptable value |

**Important:** In actual software performance testing, **a lower response time is normally better. A 350 ms response is not a quality defect. The artificial LSL of 400 ms is introduced here to demonstrate** both upper and lower specification violations. It could alternatively represent an agreed two-sided performance envelope for a benchmark test.

**2. Phase I — Establishing control limits**

**Step 1. Collect the initial 30 data points**

**Step 2. Compute initial control limits**

x̄ = ( Σ_{i=1}^{n} x_i ) / n

s = √( Σ_{i=1}^{n} (x_i − x̄)² / (n − 1) )

UCL = x̄ + 3s
LCL = x̄ − 3s

|---|---|
| Mean | 499.57 ms |
| Sample SD | 40.05 ms |

| LCL | 379.42 ms |

- **Control chart (cont.)**

*(Screenshot of the worked example, continued; key phrases are underlined in green, shown here in bold)*

**Step 3. Investigate and clean abnormal observations**

Suppose investigation reveals:

- **Observation 15 (650 ms):** A backup job accidentally ran during the benchmark, creating abnormal server load.
- **Observation 30 (350 ms):** The test bypassed the database because a mock service was accidentally enabled.

**These are assignable causes. The team corrects the test configuration and excludes the two invalid baseline observations**, documenting the reasons.

**Outliers should not be removed merely because they fall outside the control limits. If an extreme observation reflects the actual software process, it is evidence of a problem, not data to discard.**

**Step 4. Recompute control limits**

Using the **remaining 28 observations**:

| Mean | Sample SD | Baseline observations |
|---|---|---|
| 499.54 ms | 7.49 ms | 28 valid points |

The revised limits are:

UCL = 499.54 + 3(7.4857) = 521.99

LCL = 499.54 − 3(7.4857) = 477.08

The **control limits are now substantially narrower** because the abnormal observations no longer inflate the estimate of process variation.

**3. How the Flowchart Supports Quality Control**

A flowchart is one of the seven basic quality tools commonly discussed in project quality management. It helps the project team understand how activities, decisions, and rework are connected.

For example, suppose 20 pull requests enter the code review process:

- 12 pass the first review.
- 8 require corrections.
- Of those 8, 6 pass the second review.
- The remaining 2 require a third review.

The team can use the flowchart to **identify where rework occurs and measure process performance**.

One useful metric is the **First-Pass Yield (FPY)**:

FPY = ( Items passing the first review / Total items reviewed ) × 100

FPY = ( 12 / 20 ) × 100 = 60%

A 60% first-pass yield means that 40% of pull requests required at least one round of rework. This does not automatically mean quality is poor, but it gives the team a measurable starting point for investigation.

## Slide 41: Statistical Sampling

- Choosing part of a population of interest **for inspection**
  - Size of a sample depends on how representative you want the sample to be of the population
  - Sample size formula (Cochran’s formula for unknown or large population)

    Sample size = 0.25 * ( certainty factor / acceptable error )²

    - Certainty factor = how confident you want to be that there is no variation that is not included in the samples (i.e. Z score of confidence level)
    - Acceptable error =  how much error to allow (i.e. margin of error or significance level or α) = 1 - (percent certainty/100)

| Desired Certainty  (confidence level) | Certainty Factor (Z score) | Acceptable Error | Sample Size |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | 2.576 | 0.01 | 16,589 |
| 95% | 1.960 | 0.05 | 384 |
| 90% | 1.645 | 0.1 | 68 |
| 80% | 1.281 | 0.2 | 10 |

## Slide 45: Six Sigma (4 of 8)

*(Screenshot; phrases underlined in green are shown in bold, as are terms bold in the original)*

In Six Sigma, the **number of defect opportunities** refers to the **total number of chances for a defect to occur** during a process.

An **opportunity** is a **specific requirement or quality characteristic that could fail**. **One software transaction or test case may have multiple defect opportunities.**

**What counts as one opportunity?**

An important rule is that **defect opportunities must be clearly defined and consistently counted**.

For example, **a login function** might have these opportunities:

1. Correctly validate the username.
2. Correctly validate the password.
3. Correctly enforce account-lockout rules.
4. Correctly create an authenticated session.

Thus, one login attempt has **four defined defect opportunities.**

However, a project could instead define the **entire login attempt as one opportunity**, with the quality requirement being successful and correct login processing.

**Both approaches can be valid, but they produce different DPMO values.**

**Key distinction:** A defect opportunity is not necessarily a test case, a line of code, or a software function. It is a predefined chance for a particular quality requirement to be violated.

For meaningful Six Sigma comparisons, the team must use the same opportunity definitions across releases or projects.

**Example: Online shopping checkout**

Suppose an online shopping system has five quality requirements for **each checkout transaction:**

| No. | Defect opportunity | Example of a defect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Validate customer information | Accepts an invalid delivery address |
| 2 | Calculate total price | Calculates an incorrect total |
| 3 | Apply discount | Applies an incorrect discount |
| 4 | Process payment | Charges the wrong amount |
| 5 | Create order | Fails to save the order |

Each checkout transaction has **5 defect opportunities.**

Suppose the **team tests 1,000 checkout transactions.**

Total Opportunities = 1,000 × 5 = 5,000

During testing, the team **discovers 25 defects across these opportunities.**

The Defects Per Million Opportunities (DPMO) is:

DPMO = ( 25 / 5,000 ) × 1,000,000

*(boxed)* DPMO = 5,000

This means that the software process produces an estimated 5,000 defects per million opportunities.

For example, consider a mature **payment-processing service handling 10 million transactions.**

If only **20 requests fail** because of software defects:

DPMO = ( 20 / 10,000,000 ) × 10⁶

*(boxed)* DPMO = 2

This is better than the conventional Six Sigma benchmark of 3.4 DPMO.

However, this result applies only to the defined payment-processing operation. It does not establish that the entire software system is Six Sigma quality.

## Slide 48: Six Sigma (7 of 8)

*(Screenshot; phrases underlined in green are shown in bold, as are terms bold in the original)*

- **Six Sigma performance level:** A statistical quality benchmark associated with 3.4 DPMO.
- **Six Sigma methodology:** The DMAIC approach for systematically measuring, analyzing, reducing, and controlling defects.

A software project can **benefit substantially from the second without attaining the first.**

**3. Six Sigma uses the DMAIC methodology**

DMAIC is a structured, five-phase approach **for improving an existing process.**

*(Five coloured blocks in a row)* **D** Define → **M** Measure → **A** Analyze → **I** Improve → **C** Control

| Phase | Purpose | Example in software development |
|---|---|---|
| **Define** | Identify the quality problem and improvement goal | Too many bugs escape to production |
| **Measure** | Collect data to quantify current performance | Measure production defects per 1,000 test opportunities |
| **Analyze** | Identify root causes of defects | Investigate inadequate testing and unclear requirements |
| **Improve** | Implement solutions to remove root causes | Introduce automated testing and code reviews |
| **Control** | Monitor performance to sustain improvements | Use control charts and regular quality audits |

## Slide 51: Testing (2 of 4)

*(Screenshot; phrases underlined in green are shown in bold, as are terms bold in the original)*

Testing and other quality evaluation activities can be divided into two broad categories:

- **Static testing:** **Evaluating requirements, designs, or source code without executing the software**. Examples include requirements reviews, design inspections, and static code analysis.
- **Dynamic testing:** **Executing software to determine whether it behaves correctly**. Examples include unit testing, integration testing, and system testing.

During the early SDLC phases, static testing is particularly important because executable software may not yet exist.

| SDLC Phase | Testing Activities | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Requirements Analysis | **Requirements validation** | Check whether the requirement "The system must process orders quickly" is clear, complete, and testable. |
| 2. System Design | **Design review and validation** | Review the database design to ensure that one customer can place multiple orders. |
| 3. Implementation | **Unit testing** | Test whether the function calculating the total order price produces the correct result. |
| 4. Integration | **Integration testing** | Test whether the ordering module correctly communicates with the payment module. |
| 5. System Testing | **System testing** | Test the complete ordering process, from selecting food to making payment and receiving confirmation. |
| 6. Deployment | **Acceptance testing and smoke testing** | Verify that the deployed system works correctly and meets business requirements. |
| 7. Maintenance | **Regression testing** | After adding a discount feature, verify that existing price calculations still work correctly. |

## Slide 53: Testing (4 of 4)

- Testing alone is not enough
  - Watts S. Humphrey, a renowned expert on software quality, defines a software defect as anything that must be changed before delivery of the program
- Testing does not sufficiently prevent software defects
  - The number of ways to test a complex system is huge
  - Users will continue to invent new ways to use a system that its developers never considered
- Humphrey suggests that people **rethink the software development process to provide no potential defects when you enter system testing**
  - **Developers are responsible for the quality of their own work**
  - **Developers do not rely on testers to ensure quality.**

## Slide 58: The Cost of Quality (2 of 2)

- Cost categories related to quality
  - **Prevention cost**
    - Cost of planning and executing a project, so it is error-free or within an acceptable error range
  - **Appraisal cost**
    - Cost of evaluating processes and their outputs to ensure quality
  - **Internal failure cost**
    - Cost incurred to correct an identified defect before the customer receives the product
  - **External failure cost**
    - Cost that relates to all errors not detected and corrected before delivery to the customer
  - **Measurement and test equipment costs**
    - Capital cost of equipment used to perform prevention and appraisal activities
