## Slide 4: A Systems View of Project Management

- You cannot run projects in isolation
- Projects must operate in a broad organizational environment
  - Taking a holistic view of carrying out projects within the context of the organization
- Project managers need to use systems thinking and apply systems management
  - Systems thinking views interacting components that work within an environment to fulfill some purpose
  - Systems management – addressing issues in business, technological, and organizational components of a system

## Slide 7: The Three-Sphere Model for Systems Management

*Figure 2-1: Three-Sphere model for systems management* — Venn diagram of three overlapping spheres: Business, Organization, Technology. *(Venn diagram layout omitted; text content of the diagram's callouts below)*

Note (side callout): Organizational issues are often the most difficult part of working on and managing projects

Questions from the combined/overall area:
- What will the tablet project cost the college?
- What will it cost students?
- What will support costs be?
- What will the impact be on enrollments?

Questions associated with the Organization sphere:
- Will the tablet project affect all students, just traditional students, or only certain majors?
- How will the project affect students who already have tablets or laptops?
- Who will develop special applications or books for the tablets?
- Who will train students, faculty, and staff?

Questions associated with the Business/Technology spheres:
- Should the tablets be based on Apple, Microsoft, Android, or another system?
- What applications will be required?
- What will the hardware specifications be?
- How will the tablets affect various networks and speed?
- Will more power cords be required in the classroom?

## Slide 10: Organizational Culture

Symbolic frame

- Organizational culture is a set of shared assumptions, values, and behaviors that characterize the functioning of an organization
- Ten characteristics of organizational culture:
  - Member identity*
  - Group emphasis*
  - People focus
  - Unit integration*
  - Control
  - Risk tolerance*
  - Reward criteria*
  - Conflict tolerance*
  - Means-ends orientation
  - Open-systems focus*
- *Project work is most successful in an organizational culture where these items are strong/high and other items are balanced.

## Slide 12: The Importance of Top Management Commitment (1 of 2)

- People in top management positions are key stakeholders in projects
- A very important factor in helping project managers successfully lead projects is the level of commitment and support they receive from top management
- Some projects have a senior manager called a project champion (or project advocate) who acts as a key proponent for a project.
  - Informal role whose main goal is to make the project succeed by addressing different obstacles while making sure that the stakeholders are always satisfied with the project
  - Project champion role may be embodied by the project sponsor

## Slide 15: Project Life Cycle (1 of 2)

- A project life cycle is a collection of project phases (PMBOK® Guide, Sixth Edition, 2017)
  - Starting the project
  - Organizing and preparing
  - Carrying out the work
  - Finishing the project
- A project life cycle defines
  - what work will be performed in each phase
  - what deliverables will be produced and when
  - who is involved in each phase, and
  - how management will control and approve work produced in each phase
- A deliverable is a product or service produced or provided and delivered as part of a project

## Slide 16: Project Life Cycle (2 of 2)

- In early phases of a project life cycle
  - resource needs are usually lowest
  - the level of uncertainty (risk) is highest
  - project stakeholders have the greatest opportunity to influence the project
- In middle phases of a project life cycle
  - the certainty of completing a project improves
  - more resources are needed
- The final phase of a project life cycle focuses on
  - ensuring that project requirements were met
  - the sponsor approves completion of the project

## Slide 17: Product Life Cycles (1 of 4)

- A product life cycle is a process used to define, create, and deliver products.
- The Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is a framework for describing the phases of developing information systems
- Systems development projects (PMBOK® Guide, Sixth Edition, 2017) can follow:
  - Predictive life cycle, e.g. waterfall, prototyping
    - large portion of the project is attempted to clarify requirement of the entire system and then to produce a design
    - low degree of change, low frequency of delivery
  - Iterative life cycle, e.g. spiral
    - high degree of change, low frequency of delivery
  - Incremental life cycle, e.g. phased development
    - low degree of change, high frequency of delivery
  - Adaptive life cycle (or agile)
    - high degree of change, high frequency of delivery
  - Hybrid life cycle

## Slide 21: The Importance of Project Phases and Management Reviews

- A project should successfully pass through each of the project phases in order to continue on to the next
- Management reviews, also called phase exits, phase gate reviews, or kill points, should occur after each phase to evaluate the project's progress, likely success, and continued compatibility with organizational goals
- It is unwise to wait until the end of project or product phases to have management inputs
  - Many projects are reviewed by management on a regular basis

## Slide 24: Recent Trends Affecting IT Project Management (2 of 3)

- Virtual team: a group of individuals who work across time and space using communication technologies
  - Lowering costs by not requiring office space
  - Providing more expertise and flexibility by having team members across the globe work any time of day or night
  - Improving work/life balance for team members by eliminating fixed office hours and the need to travel to work
  - Increasing the potential for communications problems
  - Reducing the ability for team members to network
  - Increasing the dependence on technology to accomplish work

## Slide 26: The Agile - Scrum Framework

*(Scrum process flowchart/graphic omitted — captions and node labels below)*

- The Scrum Team consists of one Scrum Master (is accountable for the Scrum Team's effectiveness), one Product Owner (effective Product Backlog management), and Developers (creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint)
- The Product Backlog is an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product. A product could be a service, a physical product, or something more abstract.
- Team selects starting at top as much as it can commit to deliver by end of Sprint
- Sprint end date and team deliverable do not change
- The Sprint Backlog is composed of the Sprint Goal (why), the set of Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint (what), as well as an actionable plan for delivering the Increment (how).
- Potentially releasable increment
