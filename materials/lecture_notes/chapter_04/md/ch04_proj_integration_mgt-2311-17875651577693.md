## Slide 6: Strategic Planning to Help Identify Potential Projects (1 of 3)

- The first step in project management is deciding what projects to do
- Strategic planning involves determining long-term objectives of an organization
  - Analyzing the strengths and weaknesses of an organization
  - Studying opportunities and threats in the business environment
  - Predicting future trends
  - Projecting the need for new IT products and services to support the organization's objectives
- SWOT analysis
  - Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats

## Slide 7: Strategic Planning to Help Identify Potential Projects (2 of 3)

**Example: SWOT Analysis for a Mid-Sized Hotel**

Suppose ABC Hotel is a 100-room independent hotel. It currently accepts reservations mainly by phone, email, and third-party booking platforms.

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Good reputation and many repeat customers | Reservations are partly handled manually using spreadsheets |
| Experienced hotel staff | No direct online booking on the hotel's website |
| Good location near tourist attractions | Staff spend significant time answering availability inquiries |
| Existing website and social-media presence | Customer and reservation data are stored in separate systems |
| Strong relationships with local tour operators | Heavy dependence on third-party booking platforms |

| Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|
| More customers prefer booking hotels online | Competitors provide convenient online booking |
| Growing number of international tourists | Third-party platforms charge significant commissions |
| Online payment is increasingly common | Customers may switch to hotels with better digital services |
| Customer data could support personalized promotions | Cybersecurity and customer-data privacy risks |
| AI/chatbots could provide 24-hour customer service | Large hotel chains have larger technology budgets |

## Slide 11: Focusing on Broad Organizational Needs

- Projects that address broad organizational needs are much more likely to be successful because they will be important to the organization
  - Examples: improve customer service, provide better communication, improve safety, increase morale
- Important criteria for selecting projects
  - Need
    - Do people in the organization agree that the project needs to be done?
  - Funding
    - Does the organization have the capacity to provide adequate funds to do the project?
  - Will
    - Is there a strong will to make the project succeed?

## Slide 12: Categorizing IT Projects

- Categorization for selecting projects

  - Opportunities
    - Chances to improve the organization
  - Directives
    - New requirements imposed by management, government, or external influence
  - Timing
    - How long it will take to complete the project and the deadline for completing it, e.g. otherwise it is no longer a valid project
  - Priority

## Slide 14: Net Present Value Analysis (1 of 2)

- Method of calculating the expected net monetary gain or loss from a project by discounting all expected future cash inflows and outflows to the present point in time
  - Determine estimated values of costs and benefits for the project and product
  - Determine the discount rate and discounted value (present value or PV)
  - Calculate the net present value (benefit – cost)
- Depending on the organization's financial procedure,
  - First year may be year 0 or year 1
  - Discount rate may vary, often based on the prime rate (the rate at which the organization could borrow money for the project)
- Projects with NPV > 0 means the project adds values and should be considered if financial value is a key criterion
  - Projects with higher positive NPVs are preferred

## Slide 16: Return on Investment

- Calculated by subtracting the project costs from the benefits and then dividing by the costs
  - ROI = ((total discounted benefits - total discounted costs) / total discounted costs)*100
- The ROI is always a percentage and can be positive or negative
- The higher the ROI, the better
- Many organizations have a required rate of ROI
  - E.g. Minimum acceptable rate of ROI of 10%
- Also, Internal Rate of Return (IRR) can be calculated by finding the discount rate that makes the NPV equal to zero
  - The higher the IRR, the better
  - E.g. IRR of JWD project is around 100%

## Slide 17: Payback Analysis (1 of 2)

- Payback period is the amount of time it will take to recoup the total amount invested in a project, in the form of net cash inflows
  - Determines how much time will elapse before accrued benefits overtake accrued and continuing costs
  - Payback occurs when the cumulative discounted benefits equals the cumulative discounted costs (i.e. at break-even point)
  - Many organizations have requirements for the length of the payback period of an investment
    - Within the analysis period is a good sign, but risk increases toward the end of the period

## Slide 19: Using a Weighted Scoring Model (1 of 2)

- Provides a systematic process for selecting projects based on many criteria
  - Identify criteria important to the project selection process
  - Assign weights (percentages) to each criterion so they add up to 100%
  - Assign scores to each criterion for each project
  - Multiply the scores by the weights and get the total weighted scores

## Slide 22: 2. Developing a Project Management Plan (1 of 2)

- A project management plan is used to coordinate all project planning documents and help guide a project's execution and control
  - Plans created in the other knowledge areas are subsidiary parts of the overall project management plan
  - A small project (e.g. involving a few people over a few months) would not need a separate project management plan (only project charter, scope statement, and Gantt chart might be needed)
- Common elements of a project management plan
  - Introduction/overview of the project
  - Description of how the project is organized
  - Management and technical processes (including project life cycle description and development approach)
  - Work to be done (scope)
  - Schedule and budget information
  - References to other project planning documents

## Slide 27: 4. Managing Project Knowledge

- Basic types of knowledge
  - Explicit knowledge
    - Easily explained using words, pictures, or numbers and easy to communicate, store, and distribute, e.g. project documents and plans
  - Tacit knowledge
    - Difficult to express and highly personal, e.g. belief, insight, experience
    - Often shared through conversations and interactions between people, e.g. mentorships, communities of practice, workshops
- Knowledge management should be done before, during, and after projects are completed
- Buidling a culture of trust and sharing, passing on tacit knowledge
  - Lessons-learned register, describing situation and recommendation
    - Documented throughout the project life cycles by team members and archived in a lessons-learned repository

## Slide 32: Change Control System (2 of 4)

- Change control board (CCB) is a formal group of people responsible for approving or rejecting changes on a project
  - Provide guidelines for preparing change requests, evaluate change requests, and manage the implementation of approved changes
  - Key stakeholders for the entire organization should be on this board
  - A few member could rotate based on the needs of each project
- Some CCBs only meet occasionally, so it may take too long for changes to occur
  - Some organizations have policies in place for time-sensitive changes, e.g. agreement must be reached by 48-hour policy

## Slide 33: Change Control System (3 of 4)

- Configuration management is about
  - Identifying the configuration of a system at discrete points in time
    - A configuration means an aggregate of versionable items
    - E.g. specification, design, documents, data, source code, executable code, test plan, test script, hardware, disks, software development environment (compilers, debuggers, emulators), project plan, and customer requirements
  - Controlling changes to this configuration
    - Maintaining the traceability of this configuration, having a historical records of old versions to trace changes throughout the project (version control)
  - Maintaining the integrity of this configuration

## Slide 35: 7. Closing Projects or Phases

- To close a project or phase, you must finalize all activities and transfer the completed or cancelled work to the appropriate people
- Main outputs
  - Project documents updates – marked as final version
  - Final product, service, or result, including
    - Transition plan
    - Written statement that the terms of contract were met or some type of project completion form
  - Final report and presentation
  - Organizational process assets updates, including
    - Project documentation
    - Project closure document
    - Historical information produced by the project in a useful format
    - Lessons-learned report
    - Post-implementation review information (if the project achieved what it sets out to do)

## Slide 39: Considerations for Agile/Adaptive Environments

- Iterative and agile approaches promote the engagement of team members
- Expectations of the project manager do not change in an adaptive environment, but control of the detailed product planning and delivery is delegated to the team
  - Unlike traditional projects where team members may not be directly involved in integration management but focus on specialized or technical skills
  - Project manager's focus is on building a collaborative decision-making environment and ensuring that the team has the ability to respond to changes and to develop additional skills
