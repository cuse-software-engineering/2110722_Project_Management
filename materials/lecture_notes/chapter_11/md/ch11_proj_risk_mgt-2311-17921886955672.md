## Slide 5: Risk

- A dictionary definition of risk is “the possibility of loss or injury”
- General definition of a project risk is “an uncertainty that can have a negative or positive effect on meeting project objectives”
- Negative risk involves understanding potential problems that might occur in the project and how they might impede project success
  - E.g. completing work later or more costly than planned
  - Negative risk management involves possible actions to take to avoid, lessen, change, or accept the potential effects of risks on the projects
- Positive risks are risks that result in good things happening; sometimes called opportunities
  - E.g. completing work sooner or cheaper than planned
  - Positive risk management is like investing in opportunities

## Slide 6: Risk Utility (Figure 11-2)

- Amount of satisfaction or pleasure received from a potential payoff
  - Utility rises at a decreasing rate for people who are risk-averse, having a lower tolerance for risk
  - Those who are risk-seeking have a higher tolerance for risk and their satisfaction increases when more payoff is at stake
  - Those who are risk-neutral achieve a balance between risk and payoff, doing a series of analyses when making decisions

*(Three small line graphs side by side, each with y-axis "utility" and x-axis "potential payoff")*

| Risk preference | Shape of the utility curve |
|---|---|
| Risk-Averse | Concave: rises steeply at first, then flattens (utility rises at a decreasing rate) |
| Risk-Neutral | Straight diagonal line (utility rises linearly with payoff) |
| Risk-Seeking | Convex: flat at first, then rises steeply (utility rises at an increasing rate) |

## Slide 7: Project Risk Management Processes

1. Planning risk management
   - Deciding how to approach and plan the risk management activities for the project
2. Identifying risks
   - Determining which risks are likely to affect a project and documenting the characteristics of each
3. Performing qualitative risk analysis
   - Prioritizing risks based on their probability and impact of occurrence
4. Performing quantitative risk analysis
   - Numerically estimating the effects of risks on project objectives
5. Planning risk responses
   - Taking steps to enhance opportunities and reduce threats to meeting project objectives
6. Implementing risk responses
   - Implementing the risk response plans
7. Monitoring risk
   - Monitoring identified and residual risks, identifying new risks, carrying out risk response plans, and evaluating the effectiveness of risk strategies throughout the life of the project

## Slide 12: Common Sources of Risk on IT Projects (1 of 3)

- Several studies show that IT projects share some common sources of risk
  - Market risk
    - E.g. Will the new product or service be useful or marketable?
  - Financial risk
    - E.g. Will the project meet the NPV, ROI, and payback estimates?
  - Technology risk
    - E.g. Will the project use leading-edge or bleeding-edge technology?
    - Can be broken down into hardware, software, and network risk
  - People risk
    - E.g. Does the organization have people with appropriate skills to complete the project successfully?
  - Structure/process risk
    - E.g. What degree of change will the new project introduce into user areas and business procedures?
- Useful to review the WBS to see if there might be specific risks by WBS categories
  - Identifying risks based on the nature of the project and product as well as project management knowledge areas (e.g. scope, time, cost, quality)
- May review risks in previous projects

## Slide 15: Tools and Techniques for Identifying Risks (1 of 3)

- Brainstorming
  - Group attempts to generate ideas or find a solution for a specific problem by collecting ideas spontaneously and without judgment
  - An experienced facilitator should run the brainstorming session
  - Be careful not to overuse or misuse brainstorming
    - Psychology literature shows that individuals produce a greater number of ideas working alone than they do through brainstorming in small, face-to-face groups
    - Group effects (e.g. fear of disapproval, domination of other people)  often inhibit idea generation

## Slide 16: Tools and Techniques for Identifying Risks (2 of 3)

- Delphi technique
  - Used to derive a consensus among a panel of experts
    - The facilitator selects a group of experts based on the topic being examined
    - Each expert is sent a questionnaire to comment on the topic
    - Anonymous responses are returned to the facilitator who groups the comments and prepares copies of the information
    - The questionnaire and a copy of the compiled comments are sent back to each expert, along with the opportunity to comment further
    - At the end of each round, the facilitator decides if another round is necessary or if the results are ready

## Slide 20: Risk Register (3 of 5)

- Risk register contents for managing the project
  - Identification number for each risk event
  - Rank for each risk event
  - Name of each risk event
    - E.g. defective server
  - Description of each risk event
  - Category under which each risk event falls
    - E.g. Technology risk (hardware risk)
  - Root cause of each risk
    - E.g. defective power supply
  - Triggers for each risk
    - Triggers are indicators or symptoms of actual risk events
    - E.g. Users cannot get to the system
  - Potential responses to each risk
    - E.g. include a clause in the supplier’s contract to replace the server within a certain time period at a negotiated cost
  - Risk owner or person who will own or take responsibility for each risk
  - Probability and impact of each risk occurring
  - Status of each risk

## Slide 23: Risk Report

- Risk report contents for communicating with selected audience, e.g. senior management
  - Sources of overall project risk
    - E.g. Unclear requirements or changing scope, Dependence on external vendors or new technology, Limited budget or schedule flexibility
  - Important drivers of overall project risk exposure
    - Key factors that amplify or reduce the total amount of risk, e.g. Team experience, Technology maturity, Stakeholder alignment, Dependency on third-party APIs
  - Summary information on risk events
    - Number of risks, e.g. 25 active risks
    - Total risk exposure, i.e. ∑(Probability×Impact)
    - Distribution across risk categories, e.g. 40% technical, 30% schedule, 20% cost, 10% external.
    - Metrics, e.g. % of risks mitigated, Risk exposure per sprint, Ratio of realized vs. identified risks
    - Trend, i.e. whether risk exposure is increasing or decreasing over time

## Slide 25: Probability/Impact Matrix or Chart (1 of 5)

- Lists relative probability of a risk occurring on one side of a matrix or axis on a chart and the relative impact of the risk occurring on the other
  - List the risks and then label each one as high, medium, or low in terms of its probability of occurrence and its impact if it did occur
  - May create a separate probability/impact matrix for negative risks and positive risks
- Process
  - Project stakeholders list the risks they think might occur
  - Then, they label each risk as having high, medium, or low probability and impact
  - Project manager summarizes the result
    - The team combine common risks and decide where those risks should be on the matrix
  - The team should focus on the risks that fall in the high sections
  - The team should discuss how it plans to respond to the risks if they occur
- Some project teams determine numeric scores of probability and impact of a risk to calculate Risk Index

*risk index = probability of risk occuring \* impact of risk occuring*

## Slide 29: Probability/Impact Matrix or Chart (5 of 5)

*(Screenshot of a 5×5 risk assessment matrix, with likelihood definitions above it, impact definitions to its left, and rating bands below it; cells are coloured green (low), yellow (medium) and pink/red (high).)*

Likelihood scale:

- 1 – (Very unlikely): A very slim chance for this risk to occur.
- 2 – (Not likely): Low chances for this risk to occur.
- 3 – (Possible): Fifty-fifty chances for this risk to occur.
- 4 – (Probable): Good chances for this risk to occur.
- 5 – (Very likely): You can bet this risk will occur at some point.

Impact scale:

- 1 – (Negligible): This risk will hardly impact your project.
- 2 – (Low): You can easily handle the consequences of this risk.
- 3 – (Moderate): It will take some time and effort to mitigate the consequences of this risk.
- 4 – (Significant): This risk could cause long-term consequences that will be hard to recover from.
- 5 – (Catastrophic): The impact of this risk might wreck your project.

## Slide 36: Simulation (2 of 5)

- Steps of a Monte Carlo analysis
  1. Identify a problem for risk analysis and variables in the model (such as time and cost)
     - E.g. the problem is the likelihood of meeting project schedule goal (and the model is the project network diagram and the variables are the time of tasks)
  2. Collect the most likely, optimistic, and pessimistic estimates for the variables in the model (such as time or cost of each task)
     - E.g. an expert might assign to a task the optimistic estimate of 8 weeks, the most likely estimate of 10 weeks, and the pessimistic estimate of 15 weeks
  3. Determine the probability distribution of each variable
     - E.g. the expert might determine the probability of completing this task in [8, 10) weeks is 20%, and in [10, 15] weeks 80%
  4. Select a random value for each variable based on the probability distribution for each variable
     - E.g. for the time estimate of this task, randomly pick a value in [8, 10) weeks 20% of the time and in [10, 15] weeks 80% of the time
  5. Run a deterministic analysis or one pass through the model using the values selected for all variables
     - E.g. this task might have the value of 12 on the first run (for calculating the total project time)
  6. Repeat steps 4-5 many times (typically between 100 and 1,000) to obtain the probability distribution of the model’s results

## Slide 44: 5. Planning Risk Responses (1 of 5)

- After identifying and quantifying risks, the organization must decide how to respond to them
  - Basic response strategies for negative risks
    - Risk avoidance
      - Eliminating cause of a risk
      - E.g. continue using familiar pieces of hardware and software instead of unfamiliar ones
    - Risk acceptance
      - Accepting consequences if a risk occurs
      - E.g. use a backup location if the project review meeting site is not available
    - Risk transference
      - Shifting risk consequence and management responsibility to a third party
      - E.g. purchase special insurance or warranty protection for specific hardware
    - Risk mitigation
      - Reducing risk impact by reducing probability of occurrence
      - E.g. use proven technology, use competent project personnel, use various analysis and validation techniques, buy maintenance or service agreements from subcontractors
    - Risk escalation
      - Notifying higher level of authority if the risk is outside the project scope, or the risk response is outside the project manager’s authority

## Slide 46: Planning Risk Responses (3 of 5)

- Basic response strategies for positive risks
  - Risk exploitation
    - Eliminate the uncertainty associated with the risk to ensure it occurs
    - E.g. organize a news coverage and public event of the project that provides new computer classrooms for a school in need (for good publicity that could lead to more business)
  - Risk sharing
    - Allocating ownership of the risk to another party
    - E.g. form partnership with the school’s board and parent-teacher organization to share responsibility for good publicity
  - Risk enhancement
    - Increase the probability or the positive impacts of an opportunity
    - E.g. generate awareness and excitement among students, parents, teachers who might do their own advertising of the project
  - Risk acceptance
    - Not taking any actions toward the risk
    - E.g. assume the project will result in good publicity and not do anything extra
  - Risk escalation
    - Transfer risks from the project level to higher organizational levels
    - E.g. escalate risk of partnering with another firm (e.g. firm that provides learning content) to a higher-level manager who has more influence with the firm

## Slide 47: Planning Risk Responses (4 of 5)

- It is also important to identify residual risks and secondary risks
  - Residual risks: risks that remain after all of the response strategies have been implemented (the response strategies fail or are not fully effective)
    - E.g. even though a stable hardware product is used (risk mitigation), there may still be a risk that the system fails to function properly
  - Secondary risks: risks that are created directly by implementing a risk response
    - E.g. using the more stable hardware may have caused a risk of peripheral devices failing to function properly

## Slide 48: Planning Risk Responses (5 of 5)

- Additional plans
  - Contingency plans
    - Predefined actions that the project team will take if an identified risk event occurs
    - E.g. use a third party backup service to save data for the web site in case the hosting provider does not have any backup
  - Fallback plans
    - Predefined actions that are put into effect if attempts to reduce the risk (i.e. contingency plans) fail or are not effective
    - E.g. use the copy on the computer or google drive in case the backup service has gone out of business
  - Contingency reserves or allowances (or known unknowns)
    - Funds included in the cost baseline that can be used to mitigate cost or schedule overruns if identified risks occur
    - An estimate reserve, not random, based on various risk management techniques, e.g. expected monetary value (EMV)
  - Management reserves (or unknown unknowns)
    - Funds held for unidentified risks that are used for management control purposes, e.g. 5-10% of total project cost
