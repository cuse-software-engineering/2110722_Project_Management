## Slide 7: 1. Planning Schedule Management

- Elements of a schedule management plan
  - Project schedule model development
    - Model of activities, dependencies, estimated durations, resources but without calendar dates assigned.
    - I.e project schedule = schedule model + calendar dates (to start running the project)
  - Level of accuracy and units of measure
    - How accurate schedule estimates should be and time unit used (e.g. hours, days)
  - Control thresholds
    - Variance threshold for monitoring schedule performance, e.g. ± 10%
  - Rules of performance measurement
    - How team members determine the percentage of work completed
  - Reporting formats
    - Format and frequency of schedule reports
  - Process descriptions
    - How all schedule management processes will be performed

## Slide 8: 2. Defining Activities (1 of 2)

- Defining activities involves identifying the specific actions that will produce the project deliverables in enough detail to determine resource and schedule estimates
  - Activities are driven by deliverables on WBS
  - Activity list: a tabulation of activities to be included on a project schedule
    - Activity name, activity identifier or number, and brief description of the activity
  - Activity attributes provide schedule-related information
    - Predecessors, successors, logical relationships, leads and lags, resource requirements, constraints, imposed dates, and assumptions related to the activity
      - (Lead = a relationship whereby the successor activity begins before the predecessor activity has completed
      - Lag = a relationship whereby the successor activity cannot start right after the end of its predecessor)

## Slide 10: 3. Sequencing Activities (1 of 5)

- Sequencing process involves evaluating the reasons for dependencies and the different types of dependencies
- A dependency or relationship is the sequencing of project activities or tasks
  - Mandatory dependencies
    - Inherent in the nature of the work being performed on a project, sometimes referred to as hard logic, e.g. testing must be after coding is finished
  - Discretionary dependencies
    - Defined by the project team, sometimes referred to as soft logic, and should be used with care since they may limit later scheduling options, e.g. detailed design must be after user sign-off on analysis work

  vs.

  - External dependencies
    - Involve relationships between project and non-project activities, e.g. software installation must be after delivery of new hardware from a supplier
  - Internal dependencies
    - Involve relationships between project activities inside project team’s control, e.g. system testing is after unit testing

## Slide 11: Sequencing Activities (2 of 5)

- Network diagrams are the preferred technique for showing activity sequencing
  - Schematic display of the logical relationships among, or sequencing of, project activities
  - Not every items on WBS needs to be shown; only activities with dependencies need to be shown
  - Some people put start and end milestones and list all activities, while some put only summary tasks
  - Two main formats are the arrow diagramming method (ADM) and precedence diagramming method (PDM)

## Slide 14: Sequencing Activities (5 of 5) (Figure 6-3)

- Types of dependencies or relationships between activities in PDM

**Task dependencies**

The nature of the relationship between two linked tasks. You link tasks by defining a dependency between their finish and start dates. For example, the “Contact caterers” task must finish before the start of the “Determine menus” task. There are four kinds of task dependencies in Microsoft Project.

## Slide 15: 4. Estimating Activity Durations (1 of 3)

- Duration includes the actual amount of time worked on an activity *plus* elapsed time
  - Effort is the number of workdays or work hours required to complete a task and does not normally equal duration
  - E.g 20 hours of work (effort) may be spread out over 2 months (duration)
- People doing the work should help create estimates
  - An expert should review the estimates
  - It is helpful to review similar projects
  - Consider availability of resources especially human resources
  - Update the estimates as the project progresses, e.g. scope changes
- A three-point estimate is an estimate that includes an optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic estimate
  - Three-point estimates are needed for PERT and Monte Carlo simulations

## Slide 26: Critical Path Method (CPM) (1 of 2)

- Network diagramming technique used to predict total project duration
  - Critical path: series of activities that determine the earliest time by which the project can be completed
    - The longest path through the network diagram and has the least amount of slack or float
    - Slack (or float) is amount of time an activity may be delayed without delaying a succeeding activity or the project finish date
- Calculating the critical path
  - Develop a good network diagram and add the duration estimates for all activities on each path through the network diagram
    - Longest path is the critical path
  - If one or more of the activities on the critical path (i.e. critical activities) takes longer than planned, the whole project schedule will slip unless the project manager takes corrective action

## Slide 29: Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT)

- Network analysis technique used to estimate project duration when there is a high degree of uncertainty about the individual activity duration estimates
  - Uses arrow diagramming method (ADM) (i.e. activity-on-arrow (AOA) network diagrams
  - Uses probabilistic time estimates, applying three-point estimates of activity durations
  - By using the PERT weighted average for each activity duration estimate (O : M : P = 1 : 4 : 1), total project duration estimate takes into account the risk or uncertainty in the individual activity estimates

  PERT weighted average = (optimistic time + 4 * most likely time + pessimistic time) / 6

  - Applies to the critical path method (CPM) as PERT/CPM

## Slide 30: Using Critical Path Analysis to Make Schedule Trade-Offs (1 of 5)

- If a task on the critical path is behind schedule, the project manager must be aware and decide what to do
- Early start (ES) – earliest time an activity can start based on project network logic
- Early finish (EF) – earliest time an activity can finish based on project network logic
- Late start (LS) – latest time an activity can start without delaying the project finish date
- Late finish (LF) – latest time an activity can finish without delaying the project finish date
- Forward pass – process to determine the early start and early finish for each activity
- Backward pass - process to determine the late start and late finish for each activity
- Free slack (FS) or free float (FF) - amount of time an activity can be delayed from its early start without delaying the early start of succeeding activities
- Total slack (TS) or total float (TF) - amount of time an activity can be delayed from its early start without delaying the planned project finish date

## Slide 31: Using Critical Path Analysis to Make Schedule Trade-Offs (2 of 5)

- Early start (ES) and Early finish (EF) (in forward pass)
  - ES of first activity = 0
  - EF = ES + dur
  - ES of succeeding activity = max EF of preceding activities
- Late start (LS) and Late finish (LF) (in backward pass)
  - LF of last activity = EF of last activity
  - LS = LF – dur
  - LF of preceding activity = min LS of succeeding activities

## Slide 32: Using Critical Path Analysis to Make Schedule Trade-Offs (3 of 5)

- Total slack (TS)
  - TS = LS – ES or LF – EF
    - If the start of an activity is delayed but still within its total slack time, no effect on project finish date
  - TS of critical activity = 0
    - If the start of a critical activity is delayed, consider reallocating resources from activities with total slack > 0
- Free slack (FS)
  - FS = (min ES of succeeding activities) – ES – dur
- FS ≤ TS
  - FS of critical activity = 0

## Slide 40: Critical Chain Scheduling (1 of 7)

- Uses the Theory of Constraints (TOC): management philosophy developed by Eliyahu M. Goldratt
  - A chain and its weakest link metaphor – a chain is only as strong as its weakest link
  - Any complex system at any point in time often has only one aspect or constraint (bottleneck) that limits the ability to achieve more of the system’s goal
- Similar to the Critical Path Method (CPM), but it explicitly considers resource constraints
- Critical Chain = longest path in the network diagram after considering both task dependencies and resource constraints
  - Whereas Critical Path = longest path in the network diagram based mainly on task dependencies
  - Resources used in the critical chain are critical resources

## Slide 45: Critical Chain Scheduling (6 of 7)

- Project buffer – Additional time placed at the end of the critical chain, but before the project end date.
  - Any delay on the critical chain will eat this buffer and the project end date will not change.
- Feeding buffers – Additional time inserted between the last task on a non-critical chain and the critical chain so that the duration is equal to the critical chain.
  - Any delay on the non-critical chain will eat this buffer and the critical chain will not be affected.
- Resource buffers – Readiness mechanisms (not time) kept alongside the critical chain to make sure that the critical resources are available to work on the critical chain activities.
  - Can be human resources or equipment.
  - For example, days before B is scheduled to begin, the resource buffer alerts the organization: “Developer1 will be needed for critical-chain Activity B in 3 days.” This helps ensure that when A finishes, B can begin immediately rather than waiting for Developer1.
- If any activity finishes early, the time gain will be added to the buffer.

## Slide 47: 6. Controlling the Schedule

- Goals of schedule control
  - Know the status of the schedule
  - Influence the factors that cause schedule changes
  - Determine that the schedule has changed
  - Manage changes when they occur
- Tools and techniques include
  - Earned value analysis (see chapter 7 project cost management)
  - Iteration burndown charts
  - Performance review
  - Critical path method
  - Project management information systems
  - Resource optimization such as resource leveling (see chapter 9 project resource management)
  - Schedule compression, e.g. crashing, fast tracking
  - Etc.

## Slide 50: Considerations for Agile/Adaptive Environments

- Schedule management is radically different using Agile and Scrum
  - Projects that rely heavily on the critical path method consider meeting the project’s estimated completion date as a crucial component of success
  - Agile projects may not even need to estimate activity durations or project schedules; less focus on activity durations
    - The team can choose appropriate amount of work to fit each iteration
    - Product owners/customers are empowered to demand what they want in each iteration and they get to see, touch, and approve a working product.
