## Slide 6: Motivation Theories

- Involving understanding what motivates people to do what they do
  - Help anyone who works or lives with others to understand themselves and others
- Intrinsic motivation causes people to participate in an activity for their own enjoyment
  - Example: some people love to read, write, or play an instrument because it makes them feel good
- Extrinsic motivation causes people to do something for a reward or to avoid a penalty
  - Example: some young children would prefer not to play an instrument, but they do because they receive a reward or avoid a punishment for doing so
- Include
  - Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs
  - Herzberg’s Motivational-Hygiene Theory
  - Pink’s Drive
  - McClelland’s Acquired-Needs Theory
  - McGregor’s Theory X and Y

## Slide 7: Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs (Figure 9-2)

- Abraham Maslow argued that humans possess unique qualities that enable them to make independent choices, thus giving them control of their destiny
  - Developed a hierarchy of needs which states that people’s behaviors are guided or motivated by a sequence of needs
  - The order and sizes of the needs are significant
    - A person cannot consider a need without the one in the lower level being satisfied
    - Once a need is satisfied, it no longer serves as a motivator of behavior

*(Pyramid diagram, from bottom (low) to top (high); caption: "A satisfied need is no longer a motivator")*

| Level | Need | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 5 (high) | Self-Actualization | Challenging projects, opportunities for innovation and creativity |
| 4 | Esteem | Recognition, prestige, status |
| 3 | Social | Acceptance, love, affection, association with a team/group |
| 2 | Safety | Physical safety, economic security |
| 1 (low) | Physiological | Food, water, etc. |

- Frederick Herzberg distinguished between

## Slide 9: Pink’s Drive

- Daniel Pink’s book called “Drive: The surprising truth about what motivates us”
- Pink suggests that managers focus on motivators
  - Autonomy: People are motivated when they have control over what they do, when they do it, how they do it, and with whom
    - E.g. flexible work schedules, self-managed projects, or freedom to experiment
  - Mastery: People like to improve their skills and master work-related activities
    - E.g. challenging work, opportunities for training, or time to practice and develop expertise
  - Purpose: People are most engaged when their work connects to a meaningful goal beyond personal gain
    - E.g. working for an organization with a clear mission or contributing to social impact projects

## Slide 10: McClelland’s Acquired-Needs Theory

- David McClelland proposed that specific needs are acquired or learned over time and shaped by life experiences; everyone has these three needs, but one tends to dominate behavior
  - Need for Achievement (nAch)
    - Desire to excel, set and accomplish challenging goals, and seek personal success
    - Prefer tasks where they can take responsibility for outcomes, the risk is moderate (not too easy, not impossible), and they can receive feedback on performance
  - Need for Affiliation (nAff)
    - Desire for friendly relationships, social approval, and belonging
    - People high in nAff seek cooperation, avoid conflict, and prefer collaborative tasks
  - Need for Power (nPow)
    - Desire to influence, control, or have an impact on others
    - Two forms: Personalized power – seeking power for personal status or control; Socialized power – seeking power to achieve organizational or group goals.
- Measured by the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) (https://www.utpsyc.org/TATintro/)

## Slide 11: McGregor’s Theory X and Y (and Z)

- Douglas McGregor popularized the human relations approach to management in the 1960s
  - Theory X (Authoritarian, Micromanagement, Tight Supervision)
    - Assumes employees are naturally lazy, avoid work, seek security, show little ambition, and need to be controlled, directed, and threatened to perform
    - E.g. a manager believes employees will slack off unless constantly monitored and given strict rules
  - Theory Y (Participative decision making, Empowering)
    - Assumes employees are self-motivated, find work satisfying, seek responsibility, creativity, and opportunities for personal growth
    - E.g. a manager provides autonomy, encourages collaboration, and trusts employees to take initiative
  - Theory Z (Added in 1981 by William Ouchi)
    - Based on Japanese management practices
    - Emphasizes long-term employment, collective decision-making, and loyalty
    - Employees are motivated by a strong company culture, trust, teamwork, and holistic concern for employees (including family and well-being).
    - E.g. companies invest in lifetime employment and group harmony to motivate employees

## Slide 12: Influence and Power (1 of 3)

- Project managers must understand what types of influence and power to use in different situations
- Influence is the ability to alter other people’s perceptions of a situation and makes use of positive language and subtle behaviors to encourage the desired outcome
- Thamhain and Wilemon’s types of influence
  - Authority: legitimate hierarchical right to issue orders
  - Assignment: ability to influence a worker's later work assignments
  - Budget: ability to authorize others' use of discretionary funds
  - Promotion: ability to improve a worker's position
  - Money: ability to increase a worker's pay and benefits
  - Penalty: ability to cause punishment
  - Work challenge: ability to assign work that capitalizes on a worker's enjoyment of doing a particular task
  - Expertise: perceived special knowledge that others deem important
  - Friendship: ability to establish friendly personal relationships between the project manager and others

## Slide 14: Influence and Power (3 of 3)

- Power is the ability to change the behavior of others and make them do things that they might not do otherwise
  - Power is much stronger than influence, because it is often used to force people to change their behavior
- French and Raven’s types of power
  - Coercive
    - Punishment, threats, other negative approaches
    - Similar to Penalty category of influence
    - Correlates with project failure but can be effective in stopping negative behavior
  - Legitimate
    - Getting people to do things based on position of authority
    - Similar to Authority category of influence
    - Overemphasis correlates with project failure
  - Expert
    - Using personal knowledge and expertise to get people to change behavior
    - People will follow suggestions
  - Reward
    - Using incentives to induce people to do things, e.g. money, status, recognition, promotion, special work assignment
  - Referent
    - Based on person’s own charisma, the person is held in very high regard, and people will follow

## Slide 15: Highly Effective People (1 of 2)

- Project managers can apply Covey’s 7 Habits of Highly Effective People
  - Be proactive
    - Anticipating and planning for problems and inevitable changes
  - Begin with the end in mind
    - Focusing on what to be achieved, write mission statements
  - Put first things first
    - Schedule time for tasks based on importance rather than urgency, because they build long-term effectiveness
    - E.g. handling emails instantly, minor deadlines, interruptions vs. developing plans, building relationships with stakeholders, mentoring team members
  - Think win/win
    - When making decision and when resolving conflicts by developing new solutions that benefit all parties
  - Seek first to understand, then to be understood
    - Empathic listening or active listening – listening with the intent to understand other person, setting aside your personal interest
  - Synergize
    - Valuing differences in others to create synergy, creating collaborative results that are much better than a collection of individual efforts
  - Sharpen the saw
    - Renewing yourself physically, spiritually, mentally, and socially, and avoiding burnout

## Slide 16: Highly Effective People (2 of 2)

- Good project managers are empathic listeners
- Before you can communicate with others, you have to have rapport
  - Mirroring is a nonverbal technique where you subtly imitate another person’s gestures, posture, tone, or speech patterns.
  - When done naturally (not forced), it creates a subconscious sense of similarity and comfort.
  - It helps build rapport faster because people tend to like those who seem like them.
  - E.g. if someone leans forward while talking, you also lean in slightly. If they speak calmly and slowly, you adjust your tone to match.
- IT professionals need to develop empathic listening and other people skills to improve relationships with users and other stakeholders

## Slide 17: Emotional Intelligence

- Howard Gardner’s book *Frames of Mind: The Theory of Multiple Intelligences* introduced the concept of using more than one way to think of and measure human intelligence
  - Gardner suggested the need to develop both interpersonal intelligence (capacity to understand the motivations, intentions, and desires of others) and intrapersonal intelligence (capacity to understand oneself, one’s feelings, and motivations)
    - Interpersonal intelligence example: empathic listening
    - Intrapersonal intelligence example: knowing that you have a high need for achievement
  - Emotional intelligence (EI) is knowing and managing one’s own emotions and understanding the emotions of others for improved performance
    - Project manager uses EI when working on team building, collaboration, negotiation, and relationship development

## Slide 19: Leadership Styles (1/2)

- Coercive (Commanding) “Do what I say”
  - Is effective in a turnaround situation, a natural disaster, or when working with problem employees.
  - Inhibits organization’s flexibility and dampens employee’s motivation.
- Authoritative (Visionary) “Come with me”
  - The leader states the goal but gives people the freedom to choose their own means of achieving it.
  - Works well when a business is adrift.
  - Is less effective when the leader work with a team who are more experienced.
- Affiliative “People come first”
  - Useful for building team harmony or increasing morale when the team has experienced conflict, stress, or difficult circumstances.
  - Focus on praise can allow poor performance to go uncorrected.
  - The leader rarely offers advice which often leaves employees in a quandary.

## Slide 20: Leadership Styles (2/2)

- Democratic
  - The leader may establish the overall goals or framework but encourages team members to share their opinions, participate in decisions, and take collective responsibility.
  - Creates a stronger sense of ownership and commitment and brings together diverse ideas and perspectives.
  - Sometimes resulting in endless meetings and confused employees who feel leaderless.
- Pacesetting
  - The leader sets high performance standards and exemplifies them himself.
  - Expects team members to work quickly, produce high-quality results, and meet the standards.
  - Positive impact on self-motivated and highly competent employees.
  - Other employees tend to feel overwhelmed by the leader’s demands for excellence.
- Coaching
  - Focuses on developing and strengthening the capabilities of individuals and the team for current roles and future careers, rather than simply directing on immediate work-related tasks.
  - Works well when employees are aware of their weaknesses and want to improve, but not when they are resistant to changing their ways.

## Slide 23: Project Resource Management

- Making the most effective use of the human and physical resources involved with a project
- Project resource management processes

1. Planning resource management
   - Deciding how to estimate, acquire, manage, and use project resources
2. Estimating activity resources
   - Estimating human and physical resources needed to complete project work
3. Acquiring resources
   - Obtaining team members, facilities, equipment, materials, supplies, and other resources as needed
4. Developing the project team
   - Building individual and group skills
5. Managing the project team
   - Tracking team member performance, motivating team members, providing feedback, resolving issues and conflicts, coordinating changes
6. Controlling resources
   - Ensuring that physical resources are available as planned, monitoring planned versus actual resource utilization, taking corrective actions when needed

## Slide 38: Resource Leveling (1 of 2)

- Technique for resolving resource conflicts by delaying tasks
  - Main purpose is to create a smoother distribution of resource usage
  - Try to shift tasks within their slack allowance to resolve overallocation or underallocation
  - Sometimes you need to delay project finish date though
- Benefits
  - When resources are used on a more constant basis, they are easier to manage
  - May enable project managers to use a just-in-time inventory type of policy for subcontractors or other expensive resources, instead of hiring more (which is usually more expensive)
  - Results in fewer problems for project personnel and accounting department
  - Often improves morale as people like to have stability in their jobs

## Slide 49: Conflict Management (1 of 4)

- Blake and Mouton’s five modes or strategies for handling conflicts
  - Confrontation (or problem-solving), Collaborating
    - Directly addressing the conflict by bringing the parties together to openly discuss differences and work toward a solution that satisfies both sides. (win-win)
    - Works best when the issue is important and long-term, and maintaining trust and collaboration is critical.
  - Compromise (or reconciliation)
    - Each party gives up part of what they want to reach an agreement that is acceptable (but not ideal) for everyone. (lose-lose or win some, lose-some)
    - Works best when a quick resolution is needed, and both sides have equal power.
  - Smoothing (or accommodating)
    - Emphasizing areas of agreement and downplaying areas of disagreement in order to reduce tension and maintain harmony in the relationship
    - One side may yield or sacrifice its own concerns for the sake of maintaining peace. (win-lose)
    - Works best when maintaining relationships is more important.

## Slide 50: Conflict Management (2 of 4)

- Blake and Mouton’s five modes or strategies for handling conflicts
  - Forcing (or directing)
    - Using authority, power, or pressure to win a conflict at the expense of the other party. (win-lose)
    - Works best in emergencies where fast, decisive action is required, and when the issue is critical for business survival.
  - Withdrawal (or avoiding)
    - Removing yourself from the conflict instead of directly addressing it.
    - Postponing the conflict, leading to a temporary pause but no real resolution. (lose-lose)
    - Works best when the issue is trivial, a cool-down period is needed, or more information or time is needed before deciding.

## Slide 55: General Advice on Managing Teams (2 of 2)
