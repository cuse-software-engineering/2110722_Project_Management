## Chapter 1 — Introduction to Project Management

### Q1

> A project is a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a product, service, or result that brings about changes to the organization that owns the project.

**Answer: False.**

The slide's (PMBOK) definition contains the word **unique**, which the question omits, and it stops at "product, service, or result"; the clause "that brings about changes to the organization that owns the project" is not part of the definition. The slide separately lists "drives change and enable value creation" as a project *attribute*, but it does not restrict the change to the organisation that owns the project (a vendor's project changes the customer's organisation, not the vendor's).

Word that differs: the question drops **"unique"** (slide: "create a *unique* product, service, or result") and appends "that brings about changes to the organization that owns the project".

- Ch1 Slide 13: "A project is "a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result" (PMBOK® Guide, Sixth Edition, 2017)"
- Ch1 Slide 15: "A project … has a unique purpose … is temporary … drives change and enable value creation"
- Ch1 Slide 47: "A project is a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result"

### Q2

> The person who provides direction and funding for the project is called a project supporter.

**Answer: False.**

The slide names this person the project **sponsor**. "Project supporter" is not a term on the slides.

Word that differs: **"supporter"** (question) vs **"sponsor"** (slide).

- Ch1 Slide 15: "should have a primary customer or sponsor — The project sponsor usually provides the direction and funding for the project"
- Ch1 Slide 36 ("Who is this?"): "Someone who owns the project and provides resources and support for the project, program, or portfolio in order to enable its success." (the sponsor)

### Q3

> Project champion commits to deliver the project, assigns the right persons to the projects, helps smooth out problems that can hinder [ขัดขวาง] development progress, and motivates team.

**Answer: False.**

On the slides the project champion is an *informal* role held by a senior manager who acts as a key proponent, addresses obstacles and keeps stakeholders satisfied. Committing to deliver the project, assigning the right people and providing resources are the formal duties of the project sponsor / top management ("providing adequate resources", "approving unique project needs", "getting cooperation from other parts of the organization"). Only "helps smooth out problems" overlaps with the champion's "addressing different obstacles".

Word that differs: **"champion"** is used where the described duties belong to the **sponsor / top management** (the slide notes the champion role "may be embodied by the project sponsor", but defines the champion itself as an informal proponent).

- Ch2 Slide 12: "Some projects have a senior manager called a project champion (or project advocate) who acts as a key proponent for a project. — Informal role whose main goal is to make the project succeed by addressing different obstacles while making sure that the stakeholders are always satisfied with the project — Project champion role may be embodied by the project sponsor"
- Ch2 Slide 13: "How top management can help project managers — Providing adequate resources — Approving unique project needs in a timely manner — Getting cooperation from other parts of the organization — Mentoring and coaching on leadership issues"
- Ch1 Slide 36: "Someone who owns the project and provides resources and support for the project, program, or portfolio in order to enable its success." (sponsor)

### Q4

> Project portfolio management is the application of knowledge, skills, and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements.

**Answer: False.**

The definition quoted is PMBOK's definition of **project management** (with "tools and" also dropped). Project portfolio management is about grouping projects, programs, subsidiary portfolios and operations as a portfolio of investments to achieve *strategic* objectives ("Are we working on the right projects?"), not about meeting individual project requirements.

Word that differs: **"Project portfolio management"** (question) vs **"Project management"** (slide); the question also omits **"tools and"** from "knowledge, skills, tools and techniques".

- Ch1 Slide 20: "Project management is "the application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements" (PMBOK® Guide, Sixth Edition, 2017)"
- Ch1 Slide 30: "A portfolio is "projects, programs, subsidiary portfolios, and operations managed as a group to achieve strategic objectives" (OPM3®, 2003) — As part of project portfolio management, organizations group and manage projects and programs as a portfolio of investments that contribute to the entire enterprise's success"
- Ch1 Slide 31: "Project portfolio management — Are we working on the right projects? — Are we investing in the right areas? … Project management — Are we carrying out projects well? — Are projects on time and on budget?"

### Q5

> Several application development projects in the same functional group might be best managed as part of a program.

**Answer: True.**

A program is a group of related projects managed in a coordinated manner to obtain benefits not available from managing them individually, and grouping is "often more economical". The slide's own examples of IT programs include an "Applications development" program made of several projects.

- Ch1 Slide 29: "A program is "a group of related projects, subsidiary programs, and program activities managed in a coordinated manner to obtain benefits and control not available from managing them individually" (PMBOK® Guide — Sixth Edition, 2017) — Grouping is often more economical as it helps streamline management, staffing, purchasing, and other work. — Examples of common programs in the IT field include … Applications development: update ERP system, buy billing system, develop new CRM capability"
- Ch1 Slide 47: "A program is a group of related projects managed in a coordinated way"

### Q6

> The quadruple constraints of project management consist of meeting the scope, time, cost, and benefit goals.

**Answer: False.**

The triple constraint is scope, time and cost; the fourth constraint that makes it "quadruple" on the slides is **quality**, not benefit.

Word that differs: **"benefit"** (question) vs **"quality"** (slide).

- Ch1 Slide 19: "Making trade-off between quadruple constraints … Labels: Quality, Scope, Cost, Time"
- Ch1 Slide 20: "Project managers strive to meet the triple/quadruple constraints (project scope, time, cost, and quality goals) and also facilitate the entire process to meet the needs and expectations of project stakeholders"

### Q7

### Q8

> Gantt chart, progress report, and project management software are among the essential tools that are widely used and have the high potential for improving project success.

**Answer: True.**

"Widely used and have the high potential for improving project success" is the definition of the textbook's **super tools** (high use + high potential for improving success). All three tools named appear in the "Super Tools" column of the slide table: project management software (integration), Gantt charts (schedule), progress reports (communications).

- Ch1 Slide 25 (table, Super Tools column): "Integration management … Super Tools: Project management software, Change requests, Lessons-learned reports"; "Schedule management … Super Tools: Gantt charts"
- Ch1 Slide 26 (table, Super Tools column): "Communications management … Super Tools: Kick-off meetings, Progress reports"
- Ch1 Slide 24: "Project management tools and techniques assist project managers and their teams in various aspects of project management — Some specific ones include … Gantt charts, network diagrams, critical path analysis, critical chain scheduling (schedule or time)"

### Q9

> For a project to be considered successful, it must meet its scope, time, and cost goals.

**Answer: False.**

The slide lists meeting scope, time and cost goals as only *one of several* ways to define project success; a project can also be judged successful because it satisfied the customer/sponsor or because its results met the main objective. The "must" in the question turns one optional definition into a necessary condition, which the slide does not say.

Word that differs: the slide says "several ways to define project success", the question says it **"must"** meet scope, time and cost.

- Ch1 Slide 27: "There are several ways to define project success: — The project met scope, time, and cost goals — The project satisfied the customer/sponsor — The results of the project met its main objective, such as making or saving a certain amount of money, providing a good return on investment, or simply making the sponsors happy"
- Ch1 Slide 28 (Standish CHAOS 2015, What Helps Projects Succeed?): "Executive sponsorship 15 … User involvement 15 …" (success factors, none of which is "met scope/time/cost")

### Q13

> You and a colleague work as project managers for a company. You see your colleague's work and you feel familiar with it. You feel that it may have been based on documentation that is the property of another company. In this case, you can immediately file an ethic complaint to the PMI.

**Answer: False.**

The Code's mandatory Responsibility standard says ethics complaints are filed *only when substantiated by facts*, and its comment adds that we abstain from accusing others of misconduct when we do not have all the facts; the Respect standard likewise forbids spreading unconfirmed information. The scenario rests on a feeling that the work "may" be copied, so the right step is to establish the facts and bring a substantiated concern to the appropriate authority, not to file a PMI complaint immediately. (A PMI complaint also only applies if the colleague is a PMI member, certification holder, applicant or volunteer.) The ethics deck's near-verbatim-article case is this same situation.

- Code Slide 9 (2.3 Responsibility: Mandatory Standards): "2.3.3 We bring violations of this Code to the attention of the appropriate authority for resolution. 2.3.4 We file ethics complaints only when they are substantiated by facts."
- Code Slide 9 (Comment): "We also abstain from accusing others of ethical misconduct when we do not have all the facts. Further, we pursue disciplinary action against individuals who knowingly make false allegations against others."
- Code Slide 11 (3.2.6): "We avoid engaging in gossip, spreading false or unconfirmed information, and/or making remarks to undermine another person's reputation."
- Code Slide 5 (1.2): "The Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct applies to individuals who meet one or more of the following criteria: 1.2.1 PMI members 1.2.2 PMI certification holders 1.2.3 Applicants commencing a PMI certification process 1.2.4 PMI volunteers"
- Ethics Slide 3: "A project manager discovers that the website of a project management company has published a very similar article (almost verbatim) to her article. … Sample ethics cases https://www.pmi.org/-/media/pmi/documents/public/pdf/ethics/ethics-complaints/sample-ethics-cases.pdf"

### Q14

> As a project manager, you always keep your promises to your customers. In this case, you follow the professional conduct of Honesty.

**Answer: False.**

Keeping promises, i.e. fulfilling the commitments we undertake ("we do what we say we will do"), is aspirational standard 2.2.4 under **Responsibility**. Honesty's standard 5.2.4 covers *making* commitments and promises in good faith (being truthful when you promise), not delivering on them.

Word that differs: **"Honesty"** (question) vs **"Responsibility"** (the chapter of the Code that contains the keep-your-word standard).

- Code Slide 8 (2.2 Responsibility: Aspirational Standards): "2.2.4 We fulfill the commitments we undertake—we do what we say we will do."
- Code Slide 7 (2.1): "Responsibility is our duty to take ownership for the decisions we make or fail to make, the actions we take or fail to take, and the resulting consequences."
- Code Slide 14 (5.2 Honesty: Aspirational Standards): "5.2.4 We make commitments and promises, implied or explicit, in good faith." and (5.1) "Honesty is our duty to understand the truth and act in a truthful manner both in our communications and in our conduct."
- Ch1 Slide 42: "PMI's Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct addresses responsibility, respect, fairness, and honesty"

## Chapter 2 — The Project Management and IT Context

### Q15

> In the three-sphere model of system management, a project manager should consider a project from the Business, Technology, and Information viewpoints.

**Answer: False.**

The three spheres are Business, **Organization** and Technology. "Information" is not a sphere.

Word that differs: **"Information"** (question) vs **"Organization"** (slide).

- Ch2 Slide 7: "Figure 2-1: Three-Sphere model for systems management — Venn diagram of three overlapping spheres: Business, Organization, Technology … Note: Organizational issues are often the most difficult part of working on and managing projects"
- Ch2 Slide 4: "Systems management – addressing issues in business, technological, and organizational components of a system"

### Q16

> The way meetings are run in the organization and the working hours of staff of the organization are two examples of the aspects addressed by the symbolic frame of organization.

**Answer: True.**

The symbolic frame covers symbols and meanings plus organisational culture, language, traditions and image. How an organisation runs its meetings and how many hours its staff work are expressions of its culture and traditions, so they belong to the symbolic frame (the slide labels the Organizational Culture slide "Symbolic frame"). The textbook makes this explicit in its symbolic-frame paragraph ("How do people dress? How many hours do they work? How do they run meetings?").

- Ch2 Slide 8 (Figure 2-2): "Symbolic frame | Symbols and meanings related to events. Culture, language, traditions, and image are all parts of this frame."
- Ch2 Slide 10 (headed "Symbolic frame"): "Organizational culture is a set of shared assumptions, values, and behaviors that characterize the functioning of an organization"
- Ch2 Slide 11 (Tom's mistakes, symbolic frame): "Students of some majors preferred textbooks. Email to faculty at the start of semester in September got no attention." (customs/traditions as symbolic-frame examples)

### Q17

> Projects pass through four phases of a product life cycle, i.e. starting the project, organizing and preparing, carrying out the work, and finishing the project.

**Answer: False.**

Starting the project, organizing and preparing, carrying out the work, and finishing the project are the four phases of the **project** life cycle (PMBOK). A *product* life cycle is the process used to define, create and deliver a product (for an information system, the SDLC: predictive, iterative, incremental, adaptive, hybrid).

Word that differs: **"product life cycle"** (question) vs **"project life cycle"** (slide).

- Ch2 Slide 15: "A project life cycle is a collection of project phases (PMBOK® Guide, Sixth Edition, 2017) — Starting the project — Organizing and preparing — Carrying out the work — Finishing the project"
- Ch2 Slide 17: "A product life cycle is a process used to define, create, and deliver products. — The Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is a framework for describing the phases of developing information systems"
- Ch2 Slide 3: "Distinguish between project life cycle and product life cycle"

### Q18

> When the product is an information system, the product life cycle is based on the systems development life cycle (SDLC).

**Answer: True.**

A product life cycle is the process used to define, create and deliver a product; for information systems that process is described by the SDLC (with predictive, iterative, incremental, adaptive and hybrid variants).

- Ch2 Slide 17: "A product life cycle is a process used to define, create, and deliver products. — The Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is a framework for describing the phases of developing information systems — Systems development projects (PMBOK® Guide, Sixth Edition, 2017) can follow: Predictive life cycle, e.g. waterfall, prototyping … Iterative life cycle, e.g. spiral … Incremental life cycle, e.g. phased development … Adaptive life cycle (or agile) … Hybrid life cycle"
- Ch2 Slides 18–20: "Product Life Cycles (2 of 4) … (4 of 4)" — the waterfall, prototyping, spiral, phased and agile diagrams presented as product life cycles

### Q19

> The important nature of IT projects which has a critical impact on project management is that the products, technology, and team members of IT projects are very diverse.

**Answer: True.**

The slide on the context of IT projects states exactly these three kinds of diversity (products produced, team members' backgrounds and skill sets, technologies) and notes their effect on managing the project (communication between professionals becomes challenging); the chapter summary says project managers must consider several factors because of this unique context.

- Ch2 Slide 22: "IT projects can be very diverse in terms of size, complexity, products produced, application areas, and resource requirements — IT project team members often have diverse backgrounds and skill sets — IT projects use diverse technologies that change rapidly — Differences in technical knowledge can make communication between professionals challenging"
- Ch2 Slide 3: "Discuss the unique attributes and diverse nature of IT projects"
- Ch2 Slide 27: "Project managers need to consider several factors due to the unique context of information technology projects"

### Q20

> Globalization, offshoring, and virtual teams have a direct impact on communication management within a project.

**Answer: True.**

The first issue the slide lists for global projects is communications (time zones, languages, culture) plus communication/collaboration tools; a virtual team is defined by working across time and space *using communication technologies* and is said to increase the potential for communications problems; offshoring is outsourcing from another country, which brings the same cross-border communication issues. The Chapter 1 tools table also files "Virtual communications" under Communications management.

- Ch2 Slide 23: "Globalization: issues in global projects — Communications – time zones, languages, culture — Trust — Common work practices — Tools – communication, collaboration … Offshoring - outsourcing from another country"
- Ch2 Slide 24: "Virtual team: a group of individuals who work across time and space using communication technologies … Increasing the potential for communications problems … Increasing the dependence on technology to accomplish work"
- Ch1 Slide 26 (table): "Communications management | … Status reports, Virtual communications, Templates, Project websites | Kick-off meetings, Progress reports"
